What the research says matters most

     Power and people dominate. Power problems are the largest single cause of
     impactful outages in the Uptime Institute data cited in the power section, and human
     error and missed procedures contribute to most outages. Cooling is smaller but fast,
     and water is rare but destructive when it reaches the wrong room.

                                                                 Page 1 of 33
Northmark Data Center Outage Prevention: Research & Feasibility

     Failures cascade through dependencies, with delays. The Google Paris, SK C&C
     Pangyo and NaviSite cases all turned a local fault into a larger outage because a
     downstream clock ran out before anyone acted. Modeling those links and their delays
     is the most defensible form of prediction.
     The Northmark prompt is about information, not only physics. Missed warnings and
     unclear handoffs mean the winning feature is the right incident, with a clock and an
     owner, in front of the right person.
     Cheap vibration sensing is good enough for trends. Low-cost MEMS accelerometers
     suit frequency and trend work and need calibration for absolute amplitude, so the
     node tracks change against each machine's own baseline.
     Whole-room prediction exists in research, not in a weekend. Learned airflow
     surrogates and calibrated twins report small errors, but they needed large simulation
     sets or real field data that this project does not have.

Limits to hard-code. ASHRAE Class A1 puts recommended server-inlet air at 18 to 27 °C
and allowable at 15 to 32 °C, with allowable humidity of 8 to 80% RH and a dew point no
higher than 17 °C. Liquid-cooling supply-water classes run from W17 (17 °C) to W45 (45
°C), with a 2 °C minimum (ASHRAE 5th edition reference card).

How long the room has. Ride-through depends on rack density and thermal mass, so
compute it per room instead of hard-coding one number. A 2007 Opengate study found a
data center at 5 kW per cabinet reached thermal shutdown within three minutes of a
power outage (Data Center Knowledge). That figure predates today's densities, so model
it from rack kW, room air volume and equipment thermal mass.

Liquid cooling hides trouble. Controls open valves and raise pump speed to hold
temperature, so chip temperatures can look normal while the loop's margin shrinks. Judge
the loop by the effort needed to hold temperature: flow per branch, differential pressure,
pump command and filter pressure drop (Electronic Design).

Forecasting precedent. Google trained neural networks on historical sensor data to
forecast data center temperature and pressure one hour ahead, and cut cooling energy
by 40% in testing (ACHR News). That is control optimization, not failure prediction, but it
shows a one-hour thermal forecast is a proven pattern.

Water, leaks and flooding

A leak is rarely the outage by itself. The damage comes from where the water travels next,
often into rooms nobody is watching.

Case: Google's Paris region, April 2023. A cooling-system pipe began leaking on Level 2 at
16:46 Pacific. Engineers were alerted to the flooded, burning battery room at 20:08, which
is 3 hours 22 minutes later, and the building's fire-suppression water ran out at 22:45. The
leak started in a part of the building Google did not operate, entered a UPS room and
caused a fire; the whole building was powered down for several hours (Google Cloud
incident report).

The lesson for this project is the gap between leak and awareness, and not knowing what
the water could reach. That gap is what a portable sensor plus a spread model targets.

Water does not follow a predictable path. Watts argues that water can spread well
beyond the leak's origin, so the priority is isolating the affected loop to limit the radius of
failure, using preprogrammed logic based on water volume, release rate and equipment at
risk (Watts). The model should therefore show a range of likely spread, not one confident
path.

What to model for water

     Cooling-loop flow. A pipe network with a flow rate and pressure at each segment, so a
     supply-versus-return mismatch or a pressure drop points to a leak zone. This is a
     standard mass-balance approach, not taken from a cited source.
     Floor spread. A 2D shallow-water solver over a floor-plan elevation grid that includes
     raised-floor voids, door thresholds, drains and equipment plinths. A published flood
     digital twin uses a GPU-accelerated 2D shallow-water solver, ingests water-level
     sensor data and reports cutting run time "from several hours to minutes" (Chuai-Aree,
     2026).
     What sits downstream. Link each wet zone to the equipment it would take out, such
     as the Paris UPS room. This reuses the dependency map in the cascade section.
     Sensors. Match the leak sensor to the fluid, and add distributed sensing along pipe
     runs as well as point sensors at units.

Vibration, mechanical wear and structure

Vibration is the earliest practical warning for rotating equipment, which in a data center
means chilled-water and CDU pumps, CRAH and cooling-tower fans, chiller compressors
and diesel generators. Standards turn one number, broadband RMS velocity measured on
bearing housings between 10 and 1000 Hz, into four severity zones.

These are the ISO 10816-1 default boundaries, which ISO 20816-1 superseded (Vibromera;

Trend beats absolute value. One maintenance-route guide puts it plainly: rate of change
is often a more useful indicator than the absolute number. A 5 mm/s reading in Zone B is
safe unless the machine read 1.5 mm/s six months earlier. Compare each reading with the
previous visit and the as-new baseline, flag a worsening of more than 20% against the last
visit, and record operating load every time, because amplitude changes with load
(Oxmaint). A portable sensor must therefore log which asset, which spot and what load,
or its readings cannot be compared.

What the spectrum says. Standard diagnostic rules map frequency peaks to faults
(TEEPTRAK):

     1× shaft speed: imbalance, the most common defect.
     2× shaft speed: misalignment, looseness or shaft cracks.
     Blade-pass frequency (blade count × shaft speed): impeller or fan-blade wear, or
     disturbed flow.
     Sidebands around main peaks: non-linear faults such as cracks or severe wear.
     Bearing defect frequencies: calculated from the bearing geometry.

These rules need shaft speed, which for variable-speed drives has to come from the drive
or a tachometer.

Structure and building. Raised-floor loading, rack anchoring, seismic bracing and nearby
construction also matter for vibration and physical integrity. This research did not go deep
on them, and they are low priority for the demo.

The next questions are which fault frequencies a cheap sensor can actually resolve and
where training data comes from; the sensor and data sections cover both.

Network, software, human handoff and process

The modeling idea. This is the design proposed here, not a cited method. Represent the
facility as a directed graph. Nodes are utility feeds, transfer switches, generators, UPS
strings, PDUs, chillers, pumps, CRAH and CDU units, racks and the services on them.
Edges mean "powers," "cools" or "shares a space or pipe with."

Each edge carries a delay: how long the downstream node survives once the upstream
node is lost. Walking the graph from a fault gives a time-to-impact for every downstream
node. That number is the countdown on the dashboard and the answer to "how long
before it gets worse."

Why a cheap MEMS sensor is good enough here

The ADXL355 is a 20-bit digital accelerometer with SPI and I2C and a noise density of 22.5
µg/Hz, drawing about 200 µA in measurement mode, and Analog lists condition
monitoring among its target uses. A recent conference study of low-cost MEMS parts
found them suitable for frequency-based condition monitoring but in need of calibration
for accurate amplitude, and another group found that a MEMS system calibrated once
against a higher-accuracy sensor can stand in for piezoelectric ones on electric motors.
That fits this project: the twin cares about change over time and about which frequency is
rising, not about lab-grade absolute values.

Three limits to state out loud in the demo and the write-up:

                                                                                           Page 15 of 33
Northmark Data Center Outage Prevention: Research & Feasibility

       Bandwidth. Piezoelectric sensors remain the dominant choice above roughly 10 to 20
       kHz. The node is aimed at the band where ISO severity is defined, 10 to 1000 Hz overall
       velocity, so early bearing defects that only show at very high frequency are out of
       reach.
       Drift. A MEMS part can suffer both zero-output and sensitivity drift over time, where
       piezoelectric sensors only drift in sensitivity. The fix is cheap: re-baseline on every
       visit and trend against that machine's own history.
       Unit-to-unit spread. A study that calibrated 25 low-cost MEMS accelerometers found
       their variability changes the accuracy of the diagnostic features. Using one node for
       all machines in the demo avoids the issue; a fleet would need per-unit calibration.

 Measurement discipline the software enforces

  Most of the value in vibration monitoring comes from process, which is why it suits a
  software-heavy team:

       Fixed points. The same checklist insists on permanent measurement studs because a
       magnetic base is not repeatable enough for trending. The app should require a point
       ID and show a photo of where to place the node.
       Baseline first. Without a baseline you cannot say what normal looks like for that asset,
       so the first visit only records.
       Two alert levels. Practitioners set alert thresholds 10 to 20 percent below the ISO
       alarm limit. The twin uses the ISO zone as the alarm and the machine's own trend as
       the early warning.
       Log the load. A pump at 40 percent flow and at 90 percent flow are different
       machines. The node asks for, or reads, load at the time of measurement.

 Data path

  The node (an ESP32 with Wi-Fi) captures a short window, computes RMS velocity, the 1×
  and 2× amplitudes and a coarse spectrum on-device, and posts a small JSON packet plus
  the raw window to the backend. Doing the feature step on the device keeps the stream
  light enough for hackathon Wi-Fi, and keeping the raw window means the team can re-run
  better analysis later. Confirm the sensor's configurable output rate and filter settings in
  the datasheet before fixing the sampling plan; this document does not assume a specific
  rate.

rest of the facility, and say plainly which parts are live and which are replayed. That
honesty fits the "digital twins need independent validation" caveat in the next section and
is easier to defend in front of judges than a claim that one sensor covers a whole building.

Evidence that fast prediction of a whole room is real

Two recent papers show that learned surrogates can stand in for slow airflow simulation:

     A physics-informed surrogate trained on CFD runs reported a median absolute
     temperature error of 0.18 °C against the full simulation, built on NVIDIA's
     PhysicsNeMo with a digital twin in Omniverse.
     A vision-style surrogate that treats the room as a 3D voxel grid of server loads, fan
     speeds and set points reports up to 20,000 times faster results than CFD, hundreds
     of milliseconds instead of hours.

Two cautions apply. Both surrogates learn from CFD output, so they are only as good as
the simulations that trained them; the HPE group generated their training data with a
commercial tool, 6SigmaDCX. And Google's own write-up points out that each data center
has a unique architecture and a model tuned for one may not transfer to another. For a
hackathon this means the surrogate idea belongs in the roadmap and the pitch, not in the
20-hour build.

The data problem, and how to get around it

Northmark has published no data, so the build needs three sources:

  1. Simulated facility data. A small model of the room produces temperatures, flows,
     power and alarms with known ground truth, and the same generator can inject faults
     on a schedule. This is the same trick the NTU/NVIDIA work uses, generating training
     data from high-fidelity simulation, at toy scale.

  2. Replayed incident timelines. The Google Paris sequence in the cascade section is a
     ready-made script with real timestamps: leak, then a battery-room alert three hours
     and twenty-two minutes later, then suppression water. Replaying it shows exactly the

                                                                                     Page 18 of 33
Northmark Data Center Outage Prevention: Research & Feasibility

       late-warning gap Northmark describes.
    3. Public bearing data for the wear trend. There are several well-known sets, listed in a

       community catalog of bearing datasets. The NASA IMS set is useful because it
       records a natural defect history over 30 days at constant speed instead of an artificial
       scratch, and the XJTU-SY set holds complete run-to-failure vibration for 15 bearings
       under three operating conditions. Both are bearings on test rigs, not data center fans,
       so treat results as a proof of method.
  The team's own sensor adds a fourth stream, small but real, and the only one a judge can
  watch change in front of them.

 Recommended stack for the build

  Keep the models light enough to explain in one sentence each:
       Dependency graph with delays for time-to-impact. Run it as a Monte Carlo over the
       delay ranges (battery runtime, thermal ride-through, water arrival) to show a range,
       not a single false-precision number.
       Simple thermal model for the room: a lumped model of heat in, cooling out and
       thermal mass, calibrated against the simulated data. It forecasts inlet temperature
       minutes ahead and can be checked against the ASHRAE recommended range.
       Trend and change detection on each machine's vibration features: a rolling baseline,
       an exponentially weighted average and a CUSUM-style alarm, plus a straight-line or
       exponential extrapolation to the alarm zone with a confidence band.
       An anomaly score (an isolation forest or similar) over the multi-sensor feature set for
       "something looks off" cases that no rule covers.
       An LLM for explanation only. NVIDIA Nemotron turns the evidence into a handoff
       note and answers "why" questions. It does not produce the numbers.

       Backtest the wear trend on one public bearing run: report how many hours of
       warning the alarm gave before failure and how many false alarms it raised earlier.
       Score the thermal forecast against held-out simulated runs and report the error in

 Water flow: three levels of fidelity

  Water is the layer that makes the demo memorable, and it is also the one where the
  physics is easiest to fake badly. Three levels are available, and the build should pick the
  middle one and say so.

    1. Rule-based path. A leak at point A reaches room B after T minutes because of a
       known drain, tray or door, and the dependency graph records the delay. This is exact
       only as far as the facility map is correct, and it is what drives the time-to-impact clock.

    2. Height-field diffusion on a grid. Each cell passes water to lower neighbors, with a cap
       on how much can leave per step. One browser project simulates a 100 by 100 grid at 60
       frames per second on the GPU, approximating the shallow water equations with
       height-based diffusion. That is the right size for a data hall floor plan, runs on any
       recent laptop, and looks convincing. It is a visual approximation, not a flood forecast.

    3. Real shallow-water solver. WebFlood solves the shallow-water equations semi-
       Lagrangian on the GPU in the browser, and its author reports that it handles a classic
       dam-break test and reproduces urban flooding in Iowa City from elevation data.
       Celeris-WebGPU runs nonlinear shallow-water and Boussinesq equations in the
       browser and needs a WebGPU browser such as Chrome 113 or later. Either is a stretch
       goal, and both were built for open water and terrain, not for a raised floor with cable
       trays, so expect adaptation work.

  What the water layer feeds is more important than how it looks. When the wet front
  touches a PDU row, a battery cabinet or a floor-level cable run, it should create an edge in
  the dependency graph and start a clock, exactly as the Paris leak did when water reached
  the battery room hours before anyone was alerted. The picture is the explanation; the
  graph is the prediction.

 Why the heat layer is indicative, not CFD

  Real airflow prediction is the part the research literature has solved with heavy
  machinery: surrogate models trained on thousands of CFD runs, as covered in the
  previous section. A twin also only earns trust after calibration with field data. DCVerse's
  calibrated twin of a real cooling plant reported a mean absolute percentage error of 1.86%
  on total cooling plant power, and the open-source OpenDT project reports that online re-
  calibration cut its error from 7.86% to 4.39%. Those numbers come from measured data
  and months of tuning. This project has neither, so the heat slice should be drawn as
  indicative and labeled that way. Nothing in the demo should be described as a validated
  forecast.

 Rendering and performance

  A 3D hall with a few hundred racks is well inside what a browser can draw, as long as
  identical objects are batched. Instancing renders many objects that share one geometry
  and material in a single draw call, and React Three Fiber's drei library wraps it. Per-
  instance color is supported, so rack health colors are free, while other material properties
  such as textures are shared. The practical plan:

       Three.js with React Three Fiber for the scene; one instanced mesh for racks, one for
       CRAH units, and simple meshes for pipes and walls.
       Run the forward simulation in a Web Worker. Precompute frames for the selected
       scenario (for example, one frame per 30 seconds out to one hour) and let the slider
       scrub through them, so the UI stays smooth.
       Keep a WebGL fallback. Test WebGPU on the actual demo laptop early; if it is
       unavailable, the grid water model still works as a shader or as plain JavaScript on a
       small grid.

  An intuitive dashboard for this problem is not a wall of gauges. Northmark's prompt is
  about missed warnings, unclear handoffs and the wrong information arriving at the wrong

    1. What is wrong, or about to be? One incident, not forty alerts.
    2. How long do I have? A countdown with a range, and a plain statement of what the

       estimate rests on.
    3. What do I do, and who owns it? The next action, the owner, and what the last shift

       already tried.

Borrow from control-room design, not from analytics tools

Industrial control rooms have spent decades on this exact problem, and their rules
transfer well. The ISA-101 standard for operator screens is built around situational
awareness, with a grayscale baseline and color reserved for abnormal conditions, and a
"squint test": blur the screen and only the abnormal items should still stand out. One
expert panel adds an important caution: the goal is getting the right information to the
right person, and gray alone does not guarantee that. A practical guide for designers
makes the same point about prediction, noting that a layout should help the operator
foresee a pending problem, for instance with a trend graph rather than a bare current
value.

 Alert discipline is the real product

  A predictive system that adds noise makes outages more likely, not less. Alarm-
  management standards put numbers on the limit: EEMUA 191 treats about one alarm per
  ten minutes per operator, six an hour, as a healthy steady state, and ISA-18.2 defines a
  flood as ten or more alarms in ten minutes (this is a secondary summary of the two
  standards, so check the originals before quoting them on a slide). The design rules that
  follow:

       Group by cause. Use the dependency graph to fold a chiller trip, its high inlet
       temperatures and the resulting throttling alerts into one incident with one clock.
       Every alert needs an action. If there is nothing for a person to do, it is a log entry, not
       an alert.
       Show a budget. A small meter of incidents raised this hour, against a target of a
       handful, makes the discipline visible to judges.
       Redundant coding. Pair every color with an icon and a word so the screen works for
       color-blind users and for photos on a projector.

 Features that speak directly to Northmark's prompt

       Confidence and freshness on every number. A reading older than its expected
       interval turns hatched and says "stale, last seen 14 minutes ago". This targets "wrong
       information at the wrong time" head on.
       Why panel. Click any clock and see the chain: the fault, the graph path, the delays and
       the sensor evidence behind each one, plus the assumption most likely to be wrong.
       Generated handoff note. At shift change the system drafts what is open, who owns
       it, what was tried and when to look again. A human confirms it; the model never sends
       it alone. This is where the Nemotron incident investigation from the Planning Doc
       earns its place.
       Scenario preview. "What if the second chiller trips as well?" reruns the cascade and
       shows how the clocks move, which is the scenario engine from Idea 1.
       Incident history. Past incidents and their handoffs stay searchable, so the next
       person sees what happened last time the same pump misbehaved.

 Straight answers to the three questions you asked

  Can we predict the future of a data center? Partly, and the parts that are possible are
  the ones that matter for this prompt. Countdowns from a known fault and short-horizon
  trends on temperature and vibration are feasible. Exact timing of rare events is not, and a
  model with no real facility data cannot claim validated accuracy. Published work shows
  the larger ambition is real: predicting temperature and pressure an hour ahead has run in
  a live Google data center, and learned airflow surrogates report errors near 0.18 °C. Those
  results took real data and large simulations.
  Can we build an intuitive dashboard and a 3D model that predicts? Yes. The dashboard
  is the safest piece of the whole plan. The 3D model can honestly be a scenario preview: it
  moves forward through clocks and a simple flow model and shows ranges, with each layer
  labeled for its fidelity.
  Can a sensor monitor each part of the system? No, not one sensor and not in 20 hours. A
  portable vibration-and-temperature node can take trustworthy readings on selected
  machines, in survey or pinned mode, and that is enough to prove the loop from
  measurement to twin to clock. Covering a facility needs a fleet, calibration and an
  installation plan, which belongs in the roadmap slide.

 Why this scope still wins on the judging criteria

  The HackUTD criteria are innovation, technical complexity, completeness, design, impact
  and presentation. The plan scores on innovation through time-to-impact clocks and
  handoff-aware incidents, which few prediction demos attempt; on technical complexity
  through the cascade engine, the sensor pipeline and the 3D layers; on completeness
  because every claimed feature works in the scripted demo; and on impact because the
  cited outage statistics, with human error a contributor to most outages, line up with a tool
  built around handoffs. The risk is completeness if the team chases the stretch items,
  which is why the build plan below puts them last.

  sections are published figures and should be checked against the cited page before they
