How to read the evidence markers

Each value carries the kind of evidence behind it, using the markers of the project's research dossier (docs/PREDICTION-SCIENCE.md).

M Measured in a laboratory, a fleet or an incident report.
S Simulated or calculated in the cited source (for example a vendor white paper's model or CFD).
V Vendor claim, not independently verified.
A Our assumption: no source was found, so the range is deliberately wide.
calc Arithmetic done by the team, with the script in research/scripts/.

Section 1

The problem and the claim

When an alarm fires in a data hall, the operator has minutes to answer three questions: is the alarm real, how long until running services are affected, and which response should go first? CenterGuard answers each with a number computed from physics, published reliability data and a seeded statistical engine, and it states how far each number can be trusted.

Why it matters

Outages are expensive. The project dossier verified the Uptime Institute figures and corrected the pitch wording to:

"In Uptime Institute's 2025 annual survey, 57% of operators said their most recent major outage cost more than $100,000, and 1 in 5 said it cost more than $1 million."Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis 2026 (released 13 May 2026). Wording as corrected in PREDICTION-SCIENCE.md §2.9.

These are survey self-reports about each respondent's most recent major outage, not a loss model for this facility. A lognormal fit to the two exceedance points gives a median of about $149k and a p90 of about $2.7M calc; with only two points behind it, CenterGuard shows it as context next to P(service impact) and never turns it into a single "expected dollars" figure.

What was wrong before the overhaul

A science audit (dossier §1) found that the prototype's numbers looked precise but were not predictions:

The claim, stated so it is true of the build

Pitch claim
When a warning comes in, CenterGuard compares the installed sensor with a portable reference reading to get the probability that the condition is real. It then runs thousands of physics-based simulations of each candidate response. Nemotron acts as a red team, steering those simulations toward the compound failures most likely to break the plan, while every reported number comes from the simulator.

The dossier's draft said "tens of thousands". The build runs 2,048 trials per option in round 0 (2 or 3 options) plus at most two stress rounds of 4,096, about 12,000–14,000 trials per evaluation calc from server/director/evaluation.ts; "thousands" is accurate.

What "science-based" means here

  • Mechanism, not curve fitting. Heat-up comes from a room energy balance, the model form of Schneider Electric's WP179 (§3.1).
  • Out-of-sample checks. One unknown is fitted to one published peak; the other cases are predicted and misses reported (§6.1).
  • Published rates for rare events from the INL/EXT-21-65055 component database (§3.3).
  • Explicit uncertainty. Parameters without data are sampled widely and labelled assumptions (§7); intervals stay a "model range" until coverage reaches 75–85 % (§6.2).
  • An LLM that cannot move the estimate. Nemotron changes which trials are drawn, and importance weights correct for that choice (§4).
Section 2

System overview

One pipeline turns readings into a recommendation. Each step is a pure, seeded TypeScript module under src/domain/engine/, so the same input always gives the same output.

Scope note. Forecast mode is opt-in for simulateScenario and predictFix. The stage, presentation and phone screens still replay the scenario script; only the evaluate path, the director session and the ensemble always forecast (implementation notes, "Decisions").

Section 3 · Models

The models

Five models produce the numbers: equations in plain language, constants with their evidence markers, and what each model cannot do.

3.1 Heat-up after cooling loss: a room energy balance with two clocks

Idea. When the cooling units stop, the servers keep running on UPS and their heat goes first into the room air, then into the metal of the servers and racks, and, if the fans still run, into the chilled-water loop. A rack has no temperature limit of its own: its inlet is the zone's air. CenterGuard therefore models one well-mixed zone per pair of air handlers (CRAH units) with three lumped heat stores, the model form used by Schneider Electric WP179 and its patents US9418179B2 and US10817033.

Ca dTa/dt = Pair − Qcoil(t) − G (Ta − Tm)     room air (≈ rack inlet) Cm dTm/dt = G (Ta − Tm)     IT equipment and rack steel Cw dTw/dt = Qcoil(t) − Qchiller(t)     chilled-water loop Ca = ρcpV,   Cm = Nracks mrack cm,   G = kG · ε · ṁIT cp,   Qcoil = fans on ? ṁcrah cp(Ta − Ts) : 0 C = heat capacity (J/K), T = temperature, P = IT power to air, G = IT–air conductance (W/K), ε = server heat-exchange effectiveness, ṁ = mass flow of air. Implemented in thermalZone.ts.

In plain words. Right after the fans stop, only the air absorbs the heat, so it warms fast: for the fixture hall (24 × 24 × 3.5 m, 351 kW to air) the initial slope is P/Ca ≈ 8.7 °C/min calc. Within minutes heat flows into the much larger metal mass and the rise slows toward P/(Ca + Cm) ≈ 1.2 °C/min calc. The air leads the metal by roughly P/G; that lead, not the rack's own kW, sets how fast 32 °C is reached. With fans off the system has a closed form:

Ta(t) = T0 + P t/C + (Cm/C) D∞ (1 − e−t/τ),   C = Ca + Cm,   τ = 1 / [G(1/Ca + 1/Cm)],   D∞ = Pτ/Ca Fans running with chilled water lost (or only coil water left) is integrated with explicit Euler, dt = 2 s. Tests check the closed form against Euler and energy conservation within 0.5 %.

The coupling factor kG. With textbook coupling (kG = 1) the model over-predicts the late rise in WP179's simulated room by about 8 °C at 5 min. A multiplier kG ≈ 2 reproduces the published peaks within about 0.5–3.5 °C (§6.1). Physically it stands for server fans ramping up and for thermal mass the model leaves out (floor tiles, cabling, walls). Because it is fitted to two simulated cases from one vendor, the ensemble samples it as kG ~ U(1.5, 3) instead of fixing it.

Two clocks, not one "lost" time

The old model called a rack "lost" at 32 °C. That is ASHRAE's A1 allowable limit, the edge of the operating envelope, not a failure. Servers warn or throttle higher up: Dell iDRAC warns at 42 °C and goes critical at 47 °C inlet. CenterGuard reports two clocks per zone:

  • Out of envelope when the inlet crosses 32 °C (ASHRAE TC9.9 A1 allowable).
  • Service-impact risk when the inlet crosses Ttrip ~ U(40, 45) °C, anchored on the A3 (40 °C) and A4 (45 °C) allowable limits and the iDRAC thresholds. "Impacted" in every output means this second clock, or a power, water or fire loss.

Constants and priors. Published: ρcp = 1.19 × 1006 J/m³·K; IT airflow 0.059 m³/s per kW (125 cfm/kW) S WP179; IT specific heat 650 J/kg·K (US10817033); service-impact inlet Ttrip ~ U(40, 45) °C (ASHRAE A3/A4, iDRAC); DLC throttle after CDU flow stops Triangular(0.3, 0.6, 2.0) min, from 23–353 s measured by Alkharabsheh 2017 M. Published point, assumed width: rack + IT mass U(350, 700) kg (WP179 uses 545); ε ~ U(0.6, 0.9) (US10817033 gives 0.7–0.9). Fitted: kG ~ U(1.5, 3) calc. Assumed for lack of site data A: ceiling U(3.0, 4.5) m, a 0.6 m plenum with p = 0.5, inter-zone mixing U(0, 0.5), loop water log-U(3, 11) L/kW (not identifiable from WP179), coil share U(0.1, 0.3), CRAH airflow 1.2 × IT airflow, coil effectiveness 0.75, DLC share to air 0.15, start temperature 22.5 °C.

How the clocks enter the cascade. For each parameter draw θ the engine computes, once per zone, the minutes from fan loss to each threshold (toff), from loss of chilled-water flow (tflow) and from loss of chilled-water production (tchw). The dependency edges then take their delays from physics: CRAH → rack = toff, pump → CRAH = tflow − toff, chiller → pump = tchw − tflow, so a chiller loss reaches the racks at tchw. Racks in a zone share one draw, so they heat up together. The four 31 kW CDU-fed GPU racks take the measured-throttle delay instead, counted as their impact time.

What this model cannot do
It is lumped, not CFD. It cannot represent hot spots, recirculation at a single rack or containment leakage, and one rack's inlet can run several °C hotter than its zone (per-cabinet CFD in Khankari's work reaches 35 °C two to four times faster than well-mixed). Well-mixed is therefore the slow bound. The display-only curve thermal.ts inletTempAt still drives the synthesized readings on screen; predictions use thermalZone.ts.

3.2 Is the alarm real? The evidence posterior

Idea. An installed sensor says a rack inlet is hot; a technician holds a portable reference probe at the same point. Instead of a fixed threshold, CenterGuard asks which explanation makes the portable reading more likely under an explicit error budget, and turns that into a probability.

Hreal: the installed reading reflects a real condition at the point.   Hmeas: it is a measurement problem (offset, drift, placement, recirculation). p(y | Hreal) = tν( y ; xinstalled, √(σinst² + σref² + σsp²) ) p(y | Hmeas) = tν( y ; μbaseline, √(σmodel² + σref² + σsp²) ) LR = p(y | Hreal) / p(y | Hmeas),    P(real | y) = LR·π / (LR·π + 1 − π) y = mean of the portable hold; tν = Student-t with ν = 4; μbaseline = mean of the neighbours' installed readings (same row, ±2 slots) or the model baseline; π = prior P(real). Implemented in evidence.ts.

Why Student-t and not a Gaussian. Heavy tails stop one counterfeit or misplaced probe from producing absurd certainty. Counterfeit DS18B20 probes have been measured with offsets of −3.5 to +3 °C (Petrich M); ν = 4 covers that.

Verdicts. P ≥ 0.9 gives corroborated_abnormal, P ≤ 0.1 gives measurement_disagreement, anything between is inconclusive. The wording describes the measurements, never the equipment: the rationale never says a sensor is "broken" or a rack is "safe". Measurability gate. The rig's air probe cannot read chilled-water supply temperature, battery temperature, UPS autonomy, flow or pressure, and its vibration channel is a motion switch, not a mm/s sensor. For those the verdict is inconclusive with "not measurable by the portable rig", and P stays at the prior.

Worked example (the dossier's inputs: σinst 1, σref 0.3, σsp 1.5, baseline 23.5 ± 1.5 °C, installed 34.2 °C). A portable reading of 24.0 °C gives P(real) = 0.0053, so the readings favour a measurement problem. A reading of 31.0 °C gives P(real) = 0.90 (LR ≈ 9.4). The dossier's Gaussian sketch had quoted LR ≈ 110 for the second case; the Student-t likelihood is deliberately more cautious, and the test asserts P > 0.88. For water, a wet probe at π = 0.5 gives P(real) = 0.95 / (0.95 + 0.02) = 0.979.

How P(real) moves the forecast. P(real) becomes the probability of the hypothesis "the alarm is real" in the ensemble (§3.5), so a disagreeing portable reading lowers the predicted impact instead of being ignored. (Probe-settling extrapolation and a slope term in the likelihood are future work, §7.)

3.3 Backup equipment can fail: reliability priors from INL

Idea. A response plan usually depends on backup equipment working when asked: the generator starts, the transfer switch transfers, the standby air handler starts, the chiller restarts. Each of those has a published failure probability per demand. The source is Idaho National Laboratory's Industry-Average Performance for Components and Initiating Events at U.S. Commercial Nuclear Power Plants: 2020 Update (INL/EXT-21-65055) M, transcribed into research/derived/reliability_priors.json.

How they are sampled. Each rate is a Beta(f + 0.5, n − f + 0.5) posterior (Jeffreys prior) for f failures in n demands. For one demand, a Bernoulli trial with Beta-distributed p is exactly Bernoulli(Beta mean), which is what the engine samples.

Sampled per demand in the engine (priors.ts): generator fails to start 2.22e−3 (INL EDG-FTS, Beta(136.5, 61227.5)); ATS fails to transfer 1.33e−3 (INL ABT-FF); UPS drops load on transfer 1.0e−3 (Schneider WP75 V); standby CRAH fails to start 2.71e−3 (INL AHU-FTS-NS 2.11e−4 plus 2.5e−3 maintenance unavailability); chiller fails to restart 2.51e−2 (INL CHL-FTS-NR 5.09e−3 plus 2.0e−2 unavailable).

Unobserved future faults as hypotheses. A scripted fault that nobody could see yet becomes a hypothesis whose prior is the chance of failing during the remaining horizon, 1 − exp(−rate × hours). The per-hour rates are INL means: chiller fail-to-run 3.87e−5/h, air handler 2.61e−6/h, pump 2.26e−6/h, inverter (UPS) 3.49e−6/h, battery 4.05e−7/h, breaker spurious opening 1.73e−7/h, ATS spurious 1.25e−7/h, generator fail-to-run while running 3.31e−3/h, manual-valve small external leak 8.67e−8/h. For the chiller incident, "CH-2 also trips" over the remaining 58 minutes therefore has a prior of about 3.7e−5 calc. If a caution-level precursor is already active (CRAH-2 vibration), P(trip within the horizon) = 0.02, range 0.005–0.1 A: no P-F interval data were found.

Why rare events need care. At 2.2e−3, a 400-run Monte Carlo sees on average about 0.8 generator failures. Plain sampling cannot estimate those tails well, which is the reason for the importance sampler of §4.

Caveat. Nuclear-plant equipment is heavily tested and maintained, so these rates are optimistic for commercial sites. The 1.65e−2 Hong Kong figure is the "poorly maintained" bound; it is recorded in the JSON but has not yet been run as a sensitivity case (not measured).

3.4 Water: the leak model

Idea. A chilled-water leak spreads across the floor and fails whatever it reaches (UPS, batteries, PDUs). The water layer (water.ts) is a height-field model on a 0.5 m grid: each step, every cell passes water to lower neighbours by surface height (ground + depth), mass-conserving; equipment footprints are 5 cm plinths; zones carry their own floor elevation; a source adds the leak rate in L/min; an asset is "wet" when water under its footprint reaches 1 mm. It is not a fluid solver (no momentum, no raised-floor plenum flow), and the UI keeps the "approximation" label.

Puddle thickness on a flat floor: h = 2 lc sin(θ/2),   lc = √(γ / ρg) = 2.7 mm  →  h = 1.4–4.2 mm for contact angles θ = 30–100° Orifice check: Q = Cd A √(2ΔP/ρ), Cd = 0.61  →  a 50 mm² joint gap at 3–8 bar leaks 45–73 L/min Capillary physics after de Gennes, Brochard-Wyart and Quéré (2004); orifice table in dossier §2.3 calc.

The fixed 4 mm film in water.ts is therefore the sealed-floor upper end, and the scenario's 38 L/min is consistent with a joint failure. In the ensemble, the rate is observed as the make-up-water flow mismatch, so only the meter error is sampled: multiplier LogNormal(median 1, σln 0.15) A. The film is U(1.5, 4.0) mm from the capillary formula. The start is unobserved: time inferred − U(0, 10) min A. Arrival times come from cached water-layer runs (no isolation, or isolation after 3, 6, 10, 15, 22 or 30 min), scaled by (film / 4 mm) / rate multiplier, a volume-limited front A; closing the valve stops the water at the fix time.

No foreknowledge in action. In the riser-leak scenario the leak starts at T+4 but nothing observes it. It becomes inferable at T+11, when the make-up-water flow mismatch appears, and is evaluated at T+12. A forecast at T+0 or T+4 contains no leak clocks; the tests assert this, and that a forecast is unchanged when unobserved future faults are added or removed.

3.5 The cascade and the two-level ensemble

The cascade. The facility is a dependency graph: utility → generator → ATS → UPS → PDU → rack for power, chiller → pump → CRAH or CDU → rack for cooling, and derived "shares space" edges for water and fire. Each edge has a delay. A node's loss time is the minimum over its redundancy groups of the maximum over that group's members:

tloss(n) = mingroups g  maxm ∈ g [ tloss(m) + delay(m → n) ] AND within a redundancy group (a CRAH pair fails the rack only when both units are gone), MIN across groups (any one independent path is enough). Propagated by a Dijkstra-like relaxation; fixes recover nodes from their effect time. cascade.ts was split into compileGraph + runOnce, checked bit-identical with the old runCascade on six scenarios.

Two levels of uncertainty. The old engine sampled only the edge delays, independently on every edge, so the 44 rack edges of one room averaged out and looked certain. The ensemble separates what we do not know (epistemic, shared by everything in a trial) from what varies trial to trial (aleatory):

Outer (256 draws): θ ~ q(θ): physics parameters, on-demand failures, battery health, crew speed, leak film/rate/start, hot-spot growth, hazard spread, hypotheses.   weight w(θ) = p(θ)/q(θ) Inner (8 draws per θ): the remaining triangular edge delays, generator start-to-transfer, DLC throttle time, battery runtime at load, which UPS drops on transfer Pest(F) = Σi wi Fi / Σi wi,   ESS = (Σw)² / Σw²,   90 % interval from the outer-level (cluster) variance; no events → [0, 2.3/ESS] F = any criticality ≥ 4 service with every replica impacted within the 60-minute incident horizon. Without a stress mixture q = p, except that hypotheses with p outside 0.3–0.7 are drawn at 0.5 and weighted p/0.5, so both branches are always explored. ensemble.ts.

Ingredients. Cooling edges take their delays from the zone model (§3.1). Generator → ATS is Triangular(0.15, 0.5, 3.2) min, spanning NFPA 110's 10 s, WP179's 60 s and WP75's 190 s. On a utility loss, generator fail-to-start, ATS fail-to-transfer and a UPS drop become faults at their §3.3 probabilities (which UPS drops is 50/50 A). Battery ride-through is the fixture triangle × state of health, string A U(0.9, 1.0) and string B, 11 years into a 10-year life, U(0.6, 0.85) A. A fix takes effect at t + fixture delay × a crew multiplier LogNormal(median 1, error factor 2) A (no data-center response data were found); a chiller restart at t + 3 × crew + U(2.5, 15) min; standby CRAH, chiller restart and manual generator start can fail on demand. In the rack-inlet incident, a real hot spot relaxes toward now + U(0, 12) °C with time constant U(5, 20) min; shedding load halves the excess and portable cooling cuts it to 30 % A. Fire spread speed carries a LogNormal(1, σln 0.3) multiplier A.

What each response option gets. Racks impacted at p10/p50/p90 (weighted quantiles) and mean; P(critical service down) with its 90 % interval; minutes to the first service impact and to the first 32 °C crossing; P(impact) per rack; and an expected utility U = −Σracks wcrit · 1[impacted] − λ · crew-minutes, with wcrit the highest service criticality on the rack and λ = 0.05 rack-equivalents per crew-minute A, shown in the UI. The seed is derived from the incident, the time and the evidence, so a replay is identical.

Section 4

How Nemotron is used, and why it cannot bias the numbers

Nemotron (nvidia/nemotron-3.5-lightning-30b-a3b) works as a red team. It proposes compound failures that might break the chosen response, and the engine tests them. It never produces a reported number. Its proposals change which trials are drawn; importance weights then correct for that choice, so a bad idea costs precision, never correctness.

The loop

  1. Round 0 (stress_digest, cited as stress-1). The ensemble runs every response option (2,048 trials each) and builds a failure digest: the families over-represented among failing trials (lift = P(top or bottom decile | fail) / 0.1; for Bernoulli families P(fails | fail) / P(fails)), the ESS and the estimate.
  2. Proposals (stress_test): up to 6 regions, each a label, a rationale and up to 6 terms. validateProposal checks every term against the grammar and the family list; invalid terms go back to the model with a reason.
  3. Re-weighted sampling. The engine draws 4,096 trials (512 × 8) from the defensive mixture and attributes the failure probability to the regions.
  4. At most two stress rounds, then a recommendation that passes the text validator. Without Nemotron (no key, or the static artifact) the cross-entropy method proposes the regions, with the same output schema.

The closed grammar

OperationAllowed onConstraint
quantile_windowcontinuous families0 ≤ lo < hi ≤ 1 and width ≥ 0.01: sample the family only from that slice of its prior's quantiles
forceBernoulli familiesvalue 1 ("fail") or 0 ("ok")
set_pBernoulli families0 < p ≤ 0.5
scalelog-scale families (lognormal, log-uniform)f ∈ [1/4, 4]

Families are the engine's own (for example gen_fts, chiller_restart_min, battery_soh_B, k_G, crew_mult, h:real). A family may appear once per region. Anything else (an unknown family, a window on a Bernoulli, set_p above 0.5, a seventh term) is rejected. Every allowed operation has a closed-form density, which is what makes exact re-weighting possible.

Why the estimate stays honest

q(θ) = α · b(θ) + (1 − α) · (1/K) Σk qk(θ),    α = 0.3 w(θ) = p(θ) / q(θ) = [p(θ)/b(θ)] · [b(θ)/q(θ)],    b/q = 1 / (α + (1 − α)·meank qk/b) ≤ 1/α ≈ 3.3 Pest(F) = Σ w F / Σ w    (self-normalised; consistent for any valid qk) b = the base sampler (the prior, with hypotheses stratified at 0.5); qk = region k's proposal. stress.ts, ensemble.ts.
  • Defensive mixture. 30 % of draws always come from the base sampler, so every part of the prior keeps being sampled, whatever Nemotron proposes.
  • Weight cap. Relative to the base sampler, no trial can weigh more than 1/α ≈ 3.3. By Hesterberg (1995), a useless proposal therefore costs at most about a 3.3-fold increase in variance over plain Monte Carlo; a good one cuts the variance of a rare-failure estimate. stressTest reports the largest weight seen so the bound is checked, not assumed.
  • Attribution by the engine. For each region the engine computes its prior mass ("about 1 in N incidents"), P(failure | region) and its share of the total failure probability. Nemotron reads these; it does not compute them.
  • Stop rule. A stress result is marked converged when ESS > 2,000 and the 90 % half-width is below 25 % of Pest.

Tested. tests/engine/stress.test.ts checks that weights stay within 1/α of the base sampler, that a generator or ATS failure is recovered within 3 standard errors by importance sampling with N = 8,000, and that random valid proposals average to the true value (no bias).

The text validator: Nemotron's prose cannot carry a new number

server/director/validate.ts runs on every recommendation. Each engine run's numbers are registered under its run id (run-N, stress-N), in raw, rounded and percent forms. The validator then scans the summary, next action, next-scan reason and every hypothesis:

CheckRule
Clocks"T−14", "T+44", "14 min", "12–20 min" must match a cited run's number within ±1 (rounding), or a number quoted from the inputs
Percentages"3.2 %" must match within max(0.15, 5 %) of a cited or observed value
Counts and odds"12 racks", "2 services" must match exactly; "1 in 400" within max(1, 5 %)
Run idsUnknown run ids are removed and reported
Direction"later", "more time" or "buys" next to a cited shift whose sign is negative (or the reverse) is replaced by the engine's own templated sentence

Anything that fails becomes [unsupported] and is logged as a warning. The director also has a 30 s wall-clock cap and falls back to the local planner.

Whether Nemotron's regions reduce variance more than the cross-entropy fallback at equal trial counts is not measured yet; until it is, the honest claim is that Nemotron matches the fallback's role and explains the failure modes in operator language.

Choosing the next scan: expected value of sample information

The scan suggestion used to come from an ad-hoc score. It now comes from a preposterior decision analysis (Raiffa and Schlaifer 1961), computed from the ensemble's own trials with no new simulation:

EVSI(s) = Σy maxa Σi wi Ly(i) Ua(i) / Σw  −  maxa Σi wi Ua(i) / Σw Ly(i) = probability of scan outcome y in trial i: a floor probe reads wet with sensitivity 0.95 where that trial's water has arrived, else with 1 − specificity; an inlet probe reads near the installed value where the hot spot is real, else near the baseline (Student-t budget of §3.2, five bins). U as in §3.5. Units: rack-equivalents. evsi.ts.

EVSI is never negative and is exactly zero when no outcome would change the best action. Only scans the rig can physically make are candidates. Tests check that the A-11 inlet scan ranks first for the A-11 alarm, that a floor scan outranks an inlet scan for the riser leak, and that EVSI matches a brute-force two-hypothesis calculation on a toy ensemble (0.265). An honest finding: at the riser leak at T+12, isolating the valve is the best action whatever a single floor point reads, so every EVSI is 0. The ranking then falls back to how far the reading would move P(service down) (forecastShift), and the UI says so.

Reading the backtest.

  • Textbook coupling (kG = 1) over-predicts the late rise badly: 45.6 °C against 37.3 °C at 300 s in the CFD room.
  • kG = 2 meets the CFD criteria and the Example 1 baseline and 5-minute peaks.

6.2 Are the intervals honest? Coverage

Harness. npm run validate runs 36 scenarios (6 named, 3 of them modelled on real incidents, plus 30 random) × 50 iterations, seed 1. Each iteration draws a "reality" and asks whether each realised loss time falls inside the predicted p10–p90. For an honest 80 % interval the answer should be yes about 80 % of the time. The default perturbation widens delays ±30 % with a per-iteration bias of ±20 %, shifts start times ±2 min, scales leak and spread rates ±40 %, and misses a first signal 20 % of the time (up to 5 min of extra detection lag).

What the three numbers mean.

  • 24 % before. The legacy model sampled only delay noise, so its intervals were far too narrow. By asset type, chiller coverage was 4 % and ATS 5 %; under the perturbed reality the ensemble lifts every asset type, to between 38 % (chiller) and 61 % (battery).
  • 82 % self-consistent. When reality is drawn from the same priors, the ensemble's intervals contain 82 % of outcomes, inside the 80 ± 3 % target. This is a correctness check of the weighting and the quantiles (a bug would show here), not a claim about the real world.
  • 52 % under widened priors. When reality departs from the priors (wider delays, shifted starts, different rates), coverage drops to 52 %, below the 75–85 % band. Coverage more than doubles, but the intervals are still too narrow when the priors are wrong, and the median error grows from 3.0 to 4.9 minutes.
Why the UI says "model range"
Until validated coverage lands in 75–85 % under the perturbed reality, the interval is not called a p10–p90. coverage.ts generates the UI note from the latest results: "Model range, not a calibrated p10–p90: validated coverage 52 % against the 80 % target when reality departs from the priors (82 % when it does not; validation 2026-11-08)."

6.3 First-rack accuracy

"First rack right" asks whether the model names the rack that is actually impacted first. It rose from 53 % of 1,766 judged iterations (legacy) to 81 % of 1,759 under the perturbed reality and 85 % of 1,796 under self-consistency. Predicted versus realised racks saved by a fix was checked only for the legacy model, which predicted 32.1 racks saved on average against 23.6 realised, an overstatement of about 36 %. The ensemble runs did not check fixes ("0 scenarios checked"), so fix accuracy for the new engine is not measured.

6.4 Engine tests that guard the science

Prior-predictive coverage within 72–88 %; evidence-posterior calibration on synthetic truth (each P(real) bin within ±0.05); no foreknowledge (forecasts invariant to unobserved future faults, a property test); bounded importance weights, a rare event recovered within 3 SE at N = 8,000 and unbiased random proposals; EVSI ≥ 0 and equal to brute force on a toy case; the thermal backtest, energy conservation and closed form against Euler. 282 tests passed at the end of the overhaul.

Limitations of the method

  • Validation is model against model. The coverage harness judges the ensemble against perturbed copies of a model, and the thermal backtest is against simulated published curves. The only measured transients in the data are Tradat 2018 and Alkharabsheh 2017, and neither is run as a test yet. A measured site replay is the next real validation.
  • Coverage is short of target when reality departs from the priors: 52 % against 75–85 %. The interval is labelled "model range" for that reason.
  • Lumped thermal model. No hot spots, local recirculation or containment leakage; the well-mixed zone is the slow bound. The 4-minute WP179 restart is over-predicted by about 2.5–3.5 °C, and the loop volume is not identifiable.
  • Reliability data come from nuclear plants, which are well maintained. The poorly maintained bound (generator fail-to-start 1.65e−2, about 7 times the INL mean) has not been run as a sensitivity case.
  • Water is not a fluid solver: no momentum, no plenum flow, door thresholds only through zone elevations, and an unsourced drain rule.
  • The rig's vibration channel is qualitative. It cannot verify mm/s or any ISO / AMCA zone, so vibration verdicts stay at the prior. The BV-3 thresholds recommended in the dossier are not yet in the fixture.
  • Nemotron's efficiency gain is unmeasured. The estimator is unbiased whatever it proposes, but whether its regions beat the cross-entropy fallback at equal trial counts has not been tested.
  • Source caveats. WP179 values were read by web-fetch and hand-transcribed; Table 2 was reconstructed from scrambled text and is not quoted. Opengate 2007 is known only from press coverage. ISO 20816-3 and ISO 14694 were not read directly.

Future work, from the dossier, not built

Ambient-temperature (DFW month × hour) and chiller-derate families for the heat-wave scenario; a four-mode leak-rate mixture for unobserved leaks and a drain-capacity prior with a blocked-drain probability; a two-node chilled-water loop for the 4-minute restart miss; Tradat and Alkharabsheh backtests as tests; BV-3 vibration thresholds in the fixture; probe settling on the rig and a 20-hold rig calibration to replace the evidence-budget assumptions; an efficiency comparison of plain Monte Carlo, cross-entropy and Nemotron proposals; out-of-prior coverage; fix-accuracy checks and browser timing for the ensemble; a Web Worker and a predictFix ensemble field.
